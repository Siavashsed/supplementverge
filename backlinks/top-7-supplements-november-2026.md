## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How can I tell if a trending supplement is actually backed by evidence?

Most trending supplements aren't new discoveries. They're older ingredients in new packaging, and the quickest way to see through that is to ask four questions in order.

1. Has the ingredient been tested in humans? Not in cells, not in mice. A surprising number of products rest on one animal study. If the best evidence is a rodent trial, treat it as a hypothesis, not a reason to buy.

2. Does the product contain the dose used in the trial? This is where most products fail. A label can truthfully say 'with ashwagandha' or 'contains creatine' while delivering a fraction of the studied amount. Look up the dose from the trial and compare it to the label.

3. Can you actually see the dose? Proprietary blends list ingredients without individual amounts, so you can't answer question 2. I treat an undisclosed blend as a no.

4. Did the trial measure something you care about? Many studies track a blood marker rather than performance, sleep or health. A shift in a biomarker is not the same as you feeling or performing better.

The ones that passed were mostly unexciting: creatine monohydrate, caffeine, and vitamin D for people who are genuinely low. Two last practical points. Third-party marks like NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport tell you the label matches what's inside, not that the product works, so you need both checks. And if you take medication or have a health condition, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting anything new, because several popular supplements interact with common drugs.
