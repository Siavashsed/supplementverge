**Title:** That '28% cortisol reduction' ashwagandha stat: has anyone actually looked at the trials behind it?

I keep seeing ashwagandha labels and ads quoting a 28% drop in cortisol, so I went back to the research. The number is real, but it comes from a single trial: around 60 adults, about two months, 600 mg a day of KSM-66. It's a decent study. It isn't proof that the supplement fixes stress for everyone.

Across the wider literature, ashwagandha does seem to lower perceived stress scores more than placebo. The effect is just smaller and messier than the marketing suggests. Most of the trials are short, the groups are small, and a lot of them are funded by the companies that sell the extracts.

I also ran a 12-week self-experiment with 600 mg KSM-66 and filled in the Perceived Stress Scale every Sunday night. My score went from 22 to 16. That's one person with no blinding, so I'm not claiming much from it. It was interesting to watch, though, especially after I stopped taking it.

If you take it, I'd judge it by your own tracked numbers over a few months rather than by the headline figure.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Does ashwagandha actually reduce stress and cortisol, or is it overhyped?

Both. It works, but less dramatically than the packaging suggests.

The human evidence is real. Several randomised, placebo-controlled trials show ashwagandha root extract lowering scores on validated stress questionnaires such as the Perceived Stress Scale. Some also measured serum cortisol. The most quoted result, a cortisol reduction of about 28%, comes from a trial of roughly 60 adults taking 600 mg a day of KSM-66 for about two months.

The hype comes from treating that one number as settled. A few things to keep in mind:

1. Most trials are small, often under 100 people.
2. Most are short, usually 6 to 12 weeks, so we know little about long-term use or what happens when you stop.
3. Many are funded by the companies that make the branded extracts.
4. Cortisol readings vary with the time of day, sleep and how they're measured, so a single figure can overstate a stable effect.

Pooled together, the studies point to a modest benefit on perceived stress. That's worth trying, but it doesn't replace sleep, training load management or treating anxiety properly.

I ran a 12-week self-experiment with 600 mg KSM-66 and filled in the Perceived Stress Scale every Sunday evening. My score fell from 22 to 16. That's one unblinded person, so I treat it as a reason to keep paying attention, not as proof.
