**Title:** Has anyone else noticed how small the real effect of most sleep supplements is once you look at the trials?

I've been digging into the actual trial data on sleep supplements, and the gap between the marketing and the evidence is bigger than I expected.

Melatonin is the best supported option by a wide margin. Even so, the meta-analyses put it at roughly 7 minutes faster sleep onset on average. That's real, but it's not the knockout effect most people assume. It seems to work best for shifted schedules, jet lag and delayed sleep phase rather than general insomnia, and low doses (0.5 to 1 mg) often do as well as the 10 mg gummies.

Magnesium is the one people swear by, but most of the positive trials are small, in older adults, or in people who were low to begin with. If your diet is decent, the effect may be close to nothing.

Valerian, L-theanine, ashwagandha and the blends get thinner fast: small samples, short durations, inconsistent results.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Which sleep supplements actually have scientific evidence behind them?

Short answer: melatonin, and to a much lesser degree a couple of others. Most of what's on the shelf hasn't earned its claims.

Melatonin has the deepest evidence base. Meta-analyses of randomized trials show it shortens the time to fall asleep by roughly seven minutes on average and modestly increases total sleep time. That's a real effect, but a small one for general insomnia. It does more for circadian problems like jet lag, shift work and delayed sleep phase, where timing matters as much as dose. Low doses (0.5 to 3 mg) taken a few hours before your target bedtime often work as well as high-dose products.

Magnesium is where I'd put the "barely passes" label. The supportive trials are small and mostly in older adults or people with low magnesium status. If your intake is already adequate, don't expect much.

After that it gets thin. Valerian trials are inconsistent and many are poor quality. L-theanine and glycine have a handful of small studies with promising subjective results but little objective sleep data. Ashwagandha has some positive trials, though many are industry funded and short. Proprietary blends are rarely tested as the actual product.

I wrote up a fuller ranking by evidence quality here, including a small block I ran with university soccer players comparing melatonin, magnesium and a placebo capsule: https://siavashsed.github.io/supplementverge/sleep-supplements-ranked-by-evidence-quality/

Two practical points. First, if poor sleep has lasted more than a few months, CBT-I (cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia) outperforms every supplement listed and is the recommended first-line treatment. Second, check with your doctor before starting melatonin if you take blood thinners, anticonvulsants or immunosuppressants, or if you're pregnant.

Supplements can help at the margins. They rarely fix the foundation.
