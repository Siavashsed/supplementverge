**Title:** Has anyone actually noticed a recovery difference between EPA-heavy and DHA-heavy fish oil?

EPA gets all the recovery hype and DHA tends to get treated like filler. After digging into the research, I'm not convinced either one has won the argument.

In 2022 I ran a fish oil protocol with 18 university rugby players through a pre-season block. We logged soreness scores every morning at 7:30 on a clipboard that quickly got coffee-stained. ## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Is EPA or DHA better for muscle recovery after exercise?

Short answer: neither has clearly won, and the benefit of either is smaller than supplement marketing suggests.

EPA gets most of the attention in recovery marketing, largely because of its role in inflammatory signalling. DHA is often treated as the less interesting partner. In practice the research doesn't support a confident ranking, and many studies use mixed products, which makes it hard to separate the two.

What the evidence does support is fairly consistent. Omega-3s might reduce muscle soreness a little. The effect is modest and appears to depend on dose, so a low-dose capsule is unlikely to do much. And it is nowhere near what many bottles imply.

In 2022 I ran a fish oil protocol with 18 university rugby players through a pre-season block, logging soreness every morning. That kind of data is useful, but it also shows the limits. Soreness scores are self-reported, so small effects are easy to confuse with expectation, and one group's results don't settle the EPA versus DHA question.

If you're deciding what to do:

1. Check the actual EPA and DHA milligrams per serving, not the headline fish oil figure.
2. Treat omega-3s as a small possible extra, not a replacement for sleep, adequate protein and sensible training load.
3. Give it a fair trial and track your own soreness, ideally at the same time each day.
4. Speak to your doctor first if you take blood thinners or have a bleeding disorder.
