**Title:** Is glycinate actually worth paying three times more than citrate, or is it mostly branding?

I've been comparing magnesium forms, and the price gaps don't match the evidence as well as the marketing suggests.

What surprised me:

- Oxide has the most elemental magnesium per pill (around 60%), but it absorbs poorly in head to head studies against citrate. Much of it stays in the gut, which is why it works as a laxative.
- Citrate absorbs reasonably well and is cheap. In comparison trials it raises blood and urine magnesium more than oxide does.
- Glycinate is gentle on the stomach, which is a real advantage if citrate gives you loose stools. Its sleep and anxiety claims mostly come from research on magnesium in general or on glycine alone, not from solid glycinate trials.

My take: start with citrate, switch to glycinate if your gut complains, and use oxide only if constipation is the goal. Also check the label for elemental magnesium, not compound weight. A "500 mg glycinate" capsule can hold under 100 mg of actual magnesium.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Is magnesium glycinate worth the extra cost compared to magnesium citrate?

For most people, no. Citrate is the better default. Glycinate is worth paying for mainly if citrate disagrees with your stomach.

Here is how the three common forms compare.

Magnesium citrate is well absorbed, inexpensive and has the most useful comparison data. In studies that test it against oxide, citrate produces larger rises in blood and urinary magnesium. Its main downside is that higher doses can loosen stools.

Magnesium glycinate is magnesium bound to the amino acid glycine. It tends to be easier on the gut, which is a genuine reason to choose it. The claims that it is uniquely good for sleep or anxiety are weaker than the marketing implies. Most of that evidence comes from magnesium studies in general or from glycine on its own, not from well designed trials of glycinate itself.

Magnesium oxide contains the most magnesium by weight, roughly 60 percent, but much of it is not absorbed. That unabsorbed magnesium draws water into the bowel, which is why oxide works better as a laxative than as a way to fix low intake.

Two practical points matter more than the form:

1. Read the elemental magnesium figure, not the compound weight. A 500 mg glycinate capsule may contain only about 70 mg of actual magnesium.
2. The adult upper limit for magnesium from supplements is 350 mg per day, so more is not better. Anyone with kidney disease should speak to a doctor first.

My usual advice is to take citrate with food, split the dose if needed, and move to glycinate only if your gut complains. I compared cost per dose in more detail here: https://siavashsed.github.io/supplementverge/magnesium-glycinate-vs-citrate-vs-oxide/

Food still comes first. Nuts, seeds, legumes, leafy greens and whole grains close much of the gap on their own.
