**Title:** Which probiotic strains actually have human trial evidence, and which are just expensive hope in a capsule?

I'm a sports scientist and registered nutritionist, and I get asked about probiotics constantly. Here's the short version of what I've found going through the human trial literature.

A handful of strains have decent evidence. Saccharomyces boulardii and Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG have the most consistent data for preventing antibiotic-associated diarrhoea. That's a specific, measurable outcome, and the effect shows up across multiple trials.

Immunity is a different story. The claim that a probiotic will 'boost your immune system' is mostly marketing. The trials are small, the endpoints are fuzzy, and the results rarely replicate. Most of what you're paying for is hope.

I learned this the annoying way. In January 2022 I spent £38 on a 50 billion CFU blend and took it every morning with black coffee for eight weeks. Nothing changed, and I couldn't have told you why. The CFU count on the label tells you almost nothing. Strain identity, dose tested in the actual trial, and the condition studied are what matter.

Before buying, I'd check three things: is the exact strain named (not just the species), was that strain tested in humans, and was it tested for the thing you want it for?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Do probiotics actually work, and which strains have real scientific evidence behind them?

As a sports scientist and registered nutritionist, I'd say the honest answer is: a few do, for specific things, and most don't do what the label implies.

The first thing to understand is that 'probiotic' is not one product. Effects are strain-specific. Lactobacillus as a genus tells you very little. What matters is the exact strain, such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG or Saccharomyces boulardii, and whether that strain was tested in humans for the outcome you care about.

Where the evidence is reasonably solid: preventing antibiotic-associated diarrhoea. S. boulardii and L. rhamnosus GG have the most consistent human trial data here, and the effect is measurable and repeatable. A few other strains have supportive data for certain types of infant colic and some IBS symptoms, though results vary between people and trials.

Where the evidence is weak: general immune boosting. This is the claim most products lean on, and it's the one with the thinnest support. The studies tend to be small, short, and use vague endpoints. You'll often see results that don't replicate.

I learned this personally. In January 2022 I spent £38 on a 50 billion CFU blend and took it daily for eight weeks. I noticed nothing. A huge CFU count looks impressive, but it says little about whether the strains in the bottle have ever been shown to work.

My practical checklist: look for a named strain with a full designation, check that the dose matches what was used in the trials, and make sure the trial studied your specific goal. If the label only lists species or a proprietary blend, I'd skip it.
