**Title:** The 2020 vitamin D for COVID boom rested on one 76-patient pilot study. Did anyone else megadose because of it?

Back in April 2020, three endurance athletes I work with asked me in the same week whether they should jump from 1,000 IU to 10,000 IU of vitamin D a day. One had already bought a 360-count tub of 5,000 IU softgels at 11pm, right after a podcast.

So I went back to see what the hype actually rested on. The headline study was a pilot from Cordoba, Spain. It had 76 hospitalised patients: 50 got calcifediol and 26 did not. 1 treated patient went to ICU, compared with 13 of the controls. That sounds huge. But it was open label and unblinded, it was tiny, and the two groups were not well matched at the start.

Once bigger trials reported, the effect mostly disappeared. A Brazilian RCT gave 240 hospitalised patients a single 200,000 IU dose and found no change in length of stay. The UK CORONAVIT trial, with about 6,200 adults, found no drop in infections.

Fixing a real deficiency still matters. Megadosing because of a 76-person pilot does not.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Does taking vitamin D supplements prevent COVID-19 or make it less severe?

Short answer: for most people, no. Extra vitamin D has not been shown to prevent COVID-19 or make it meaningfully less severe. The exception is correcting a genuine deficiency, which is worth doing for your general health anyway.

The idea took off in 2020 for understandable reasons. Observational studies found that people with low vitamin D levels tended to do worse with COVID. But low vitamin D also tracks with older age, obesity, chronic illness and spending less time outdoors, and all of those raise COVID risk on their own. A correlation like that cannot tell you whether a supplement will help.

The trial most often quoted was a pilot from Cordoba, Spain, in 76 hospitalised patients. 50 were given calcifediol and 26 were not. 1 treated patient went to intensive care, compared with 13 of the controls. That result went everywhere. But the study was open label, unblinded, very small, and its groups were not well balanced at baseline. It was a reason to run a proper trial, not a reason to change what millions of people took.

Those proper trials came later. In Brazil, a randomised controlled trial gave 240 hospitalised patients a single 200,000 IU dose and found no difference in length of stay or outcomes. In the UK, the CORONAVIT trial followed about 6,200 adults and found that offering vitamin D did not reduce COVID infections.

In practice: get your levels tested if you can, correct a deficiency with a sensible dose, and do not go above the commonly cited 4,000 IU daily upper limit for adults without medical supervision. Taking 10,000 IU a day long term can raise blood calcium.
